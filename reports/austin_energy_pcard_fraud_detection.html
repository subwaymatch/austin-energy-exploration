1. The combined data, and one problem that changes every total

The two releases were combined into one file, data/ae_pcard_combined.csv: 151,344 rows, every row the City released, in one set of columns. It holds 30,744 accounting lines from the older system (fiscal years 2013–2018, no dates) and 120,600 lines from the newer one (January 2018–September 2026), of which 7,959 come from the second request and fill the January–June 2019 gap. A second file, data/ae_pcard_transactions.csv, has one row per card charge for the newer system (116,842 charges).

The "Allocated Amount" column is not an allocation. Every one of the 1,716 transactions that spans more than one line carries the same amount on every line: a $103.18 grocery purchase appears on 47 item lines, each showing $103.18. The column is the transaction total, repeated. Adding it up overstates newer-system spending by $2.04 million ($65.95M instead of $63.91M). The combined file keeps the amounts as released and adds is_primary_line so that totals can be taken once per transaction.

2. Benford's Law: does it flag him?

The test uses one amount per card charge, positive amounts only, for the 76,959 transactions posted from January 2018 through September 2023 (his last purchase was September 1, 2023). Cardholders need at least 100 purchases to be scored, which leaves 170.

MAD is the mean absolute deviation between observed and expected digit shares. Nigrini's widely used guideline treats a first-digit MAD above 0.015 as nonconforming. The department as a whole sits at about 0.011, which is acceptable; Ybarra is at 0.073. His chi-square statistic is 721 on eight degrees of freedom.

That sounds conclusive, but it is not a usable rule at the level of a single card: 144 of the 170 cardholders exceed 0.015. An individual's card is a small, lumpy sample (one person buying the same things at the same prices), so nearly everyone "fails". What matters is the ranking.

On the plain first-digit MAD he is 5th of 170. The four cards above him are small (122, 331, 198, 101 purchases) and dominated by one vendor or one repeated price: on those four, a single vendor accounts for 39%–53% of purchases. MAD is larger for small samples even when nothing is wrong, so the ranking favors small cards. The size-adjusted version compares each card's MAD with what random samples of the same size from Benford's distribution produce; on that measure he is 1st, by a wide margin.

The first-two-digit test, often recommended as the more powerful one, does worse here in its plain form: rank 53 of 170. With 90 digit categories and a few hundred purchases per card, noise swamps the signal. It is still the test that shows where the problem is.

Verdict on Benford. It would have put his card on a list of five to review, and from early 2021 it does so in every quarter (section 6). It would not have singled him out, it produces obvious false alarms above him, and its statistical significance is meaningless at card level. Its best use in this case is diagnostic: the 28/29 spike turns "something is off with the digits" into a specific question about purchases just under $3,000.

What worked. Payment channel, just-under-limit amounts, weekend spending, and same-day stacking each rank him first. They work because they describe how a fictitious-vendor scheme has to operate on a card: the payee must be able to accept a card without being a real merchant (a PayPal or Square account does that), and each charge must stay under the limit that would trigger a second look.

What did not. Three tests that textbooks recommend rank him poorly, and the reasons are instructive:

  • Whole-dollar amounts (rank 39): 96% of his PayPal/Square payments are whole dollars, but the 590 routine purchases on the same card dilute the ratio. Tests run on a whole card can hide a pattern that is obvious on a subset.
  • Split purchases (rank 17): he rarely paid the same payee twice within a week. He spread near-limit payments across many payees, which defeats the usual same-vendor split test. The "same-day stacking" variant, which ignores the payee, ranks him first.
  • Payees no one else uses (rank 2, but only $96K): most of his payees were not exclusive to his card. See section 7.

Machine-learning methods. An isolation forest and a local-outlier-factor model, given ten ratios per cardholder and no labels, both rank him first. They add little here beyond the composite score, which is easier to explain to a manager: it is the average of his percentile rank on eight tests. He ranks first on four of them.

The naive baseline matters. Simply sorting by total spending puts him 5th. The four cards above him are high-volume inventory and operations buyers. Any method worth teaching should beat "look at the biggest spenders", and plain Benford (also 5th) does not.

Two things stand out without opening a single invoice. First, the medians: fifteen different payees, all billing a median between $2,650 and $2,900. Second, the merchant categories. $973,985 of these payments were coded by Ybarra to "maintenance-buildings", but the card network classified the payees as General Contractors-Residential ($358K), Cleaning And Maintenance, Janitorial Ser ($267K), Professional Services Not Elsewhere Clas ($135K), Miscellaneous Personal Services ($106K), Telecommunication Equipment Including Te ($85K) and Advertising Services ($41K). A PayPal or Square account holder picks their own category, so the category says little about the work, and that is the point: these are not merchants a card network has vetted.

The automated version of this check (flag purchases whose merchant category rarely goes with that account code for anyone else) ranks him only 26th, because "general contractor" coded to building maintenance is not unusual. The mismatch is visible to a reader, less so to a rule.

How this lines up with the auditor's figures. The auditor reports about $980,000 paid to fictitious vendors in fiscal years 2018–2023, about $750,000 approved by his first supervisor and about $170,000 by an interim one. In the data, his PayPal/Square payments total $1,026,627 (plus $55,718 in the older system), of which $771,170 was signed off by manager ID MGR-194 (2018-01 to 2022-05) and $219,097 by MGR-040 (2022-06 to 2023-06). The pattern and the dates match. The overall total and the first approver's total are within 5% of the auditor's figures; the second is about 29% higher. "Paid through PayPal or Square" is therefore a reasonable stand-in for the auditor's list, not a copy of it.

7. The same payees on other cards

This is the finding I would be most careful with in a classroom. The auditor's report is about one card. But 35 of the 44 PayPal/Square payees on Ybarra's card were also paid from 10 other cards, 367 payments totalling $836,308, and all but $2,005 of that was charged to Facility Management. Those payments have a similar shape (92% whole-dollar, 53% within 10% below the limit). After September 2023 the same payees receive one payment of $375 in three years.

Facility Management accounts for $3.20M of the department's $4.85M in PayPal/Square spending, 35% of everything the unit bought by card. A unit-level view would have raised the question before any single card did.

There are several possible readings and the data cannot choose between them: the payees could be real small contractors that the whole facilities group used (which would complicate the word "fictitious"); colleagues could have paid them at Ybarra's request or on his recommendation; or the problem could be wider than one card. The auditor's report says staff interviewed "were largely unaware of the vendors that Ybarra had utilized", which sits uneasily with these payments. Nothing here is a finding about any other cardholder, which is why they appear only as aliases. For the case study it means two things: treating "Ybarra's card" as the only positive label is probably too narrow, and vendor-exclusivity tests fail for an interesting reason.

8. What a review queue would look like

Ranking cardholders tells a manager whose statement to pull. A transaction-level queue tells a reviewer which receipts to ask for. Each purchase gets one point for each of five simple flags: amount at 90–100% of the $3,000 limit; whole-dollar amount; paid through PayPal or Square; payee used by no other cardholder; purchased on a weekend.

9. What the sign-off timestamps show

The export has cardholder and manager sign-off timestamps and an anonymized manager ID. Every one of his purchases through July 2023 has a manager sign-off; 12 from August–September 2023 have none, which fits the report's account of management starting to ask questions. Across the 71 manager IDs with at least 200 sign-offs, the typical manager records 80% of sign-offs within ten seconds of their previous one: approval is mostly done as a batch. MGR-040 does the same (89%). MGR-194, who signed off 791 of his transactions, is the opposite: only 7% within ten seconds, with a median gap of 18 seconds between sign-offs.

Neither pattern shows whether an invoice was opened, and a timestamp cannot measure attention. But it does support the auditor's second finding in a way students can verify: sign-off was recorded for every transaction for more than five years. The control existed and ran; it did not test anything.

11. Limits, and what to ask the City for

  • No labels. The release does not say which transactions the auditor counted in the $980,000. A schedule of those transactions would turn this from a ranking exercise into a detection benchmark with measurable precision and recall.
  • The purchase limit is inferred. Ask for the single-purchase and monthly limits and the dates they changed.
  • Amount semantics. Ask the City to confirm that "Allocated Amount" is the transaction total, and whether true allocation amounts exist.
  • December 2017. One statement cycle appears to be missing between the two systems.
  • Fiscal periods in the older sheet. Period 1 never appears and period 12 is double-sized; a confirmed period-to-month mapping would allow dating those rows.
  • Legal status. The auditor's report says Ybarra was indicted and arrested in September 2025. That is not a conviction, and I have not checked the outcome of the criminal case.
  • Payment-processor detection is by descriptor. A merchant is treated as PayPal/Square when its name starts with PAYPAL, PP, SQ or SQU, or the card network lists PayPal or Square/Block as the processor. Some legitimate small businesses bill this way; the test measures a channel, not intent.

12. Methods and files

  • Population. Newer-system card charges posted 2018-01-01 to 2023-09-30; positive amounts for all digit and amount tests; credits kept in the data. Split charges (IDs ending -A/-B/-C) are added back together.
  • Benford. First digit and first two digits on integer cents. MAD against log10(1 + 1/d). Size-adjusted score: (MAD − mean) / standard deviation of the MAD of 400 random samples of the same size drawn from Benford's distribution.
  • Just under the limit. $2,700.00–$3,000.00 (90–100% of the inferred limit).
  • Split purchases. Same cardholder and payee, two purchases within 7 days, each at or under the limit, together over it. Same-day stacking: two or more near-limit purchases on one day, any payee.
  • Payee key. Merchant name with processor prefix, digits and punctuation removed, first 12 letters. This merges descriptor variants of one payee and can occasionally merge two different ones.
  • Category mismatch. A purchase is flagged when fewer than 2% of other cardholders' purchases in that merchant category use the same account (object) code.
  • Composite. Mean percentile rank on eight tests: size-adjusted Benford, near-limit count, whole-dollar share, PayPal/Square dollars, sole-payee dollars, split purchases, weekend dollars, category mismatch.
  • Unsupervised models. Isolation forest (500 trees) and local outlier factor (20 neighbors) on ten standardized cardholder ratios.
  • Aliases. Cardholders other than Ybarra are CH-001, CH-002, … in order of total spending; payees are numbered. Run scripts/analyze.py --show-names to see the released names.
